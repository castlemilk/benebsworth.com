## The core idea

Heat a fridge magnet past a certain point and it stops being a magnet. Cool it back down and the magnetism returns. There is no gentle fade in between: the change happens at one sharp point, the critical temperature $T_c$, and the nearer you sit to that point the wilder the behaviour becomes. The 2D Ising model is the simplest system that reproduces this, using nothing more than a grid of arrows that each point up or down.

Every cell on the canvas is a spin, orange for up and teal for down. Each spin would like to match its four neighbours, and temperature keeps knocking it out of line. Above $T_c$ you see salt-and-pepper noise with no memory and no structure. Below $T_c$ large aligned domains form and the whole sheet leans one colour. Right at $T_c$, where this demo starts, patches of every size flicker in and out at once, and the lattice never quite settles.

## How it works

The energy of the whole lattice rewards neighbours that agree:

$$
E = -J \sum_{\langle i j \rangle} s_i s_j - h \sum_i s_i
$$

Each spin is $s_i = \pm 1$, the coupling $J$ says how badly neighbours want to line up, and $h$ is an external field that tips the balance toward up or down. The pair sum $\langle i j \rangle$ runs over neighbouring cells, with the edges wrapped around so there are no walls.

We never solve that energy directly. Instead we sample it with Metropolis Monte Carlo, a method (Monte Carlo just means we lean on random numbers) that visits each configuration in proportion to how likely thermodynamics says it should be. Pick a site, ask what flipping it would cost:

$$
\Delta E = 2\, s_k \left( J \sum_j s_j + h \right)
$$

If the flip lowers the energy, take it. If it raises the energy, still take it sometimes, with probability $e^{-\Delta E / T}$, because a warm system can borrow energy from its surroundings. That one rule, repeated about $L^2$ times per frame, is the entire simulation:

```
pick a random site k with spin s
ΔE = 2·s·(J·Σ neighbours + h)
if ΔE ≤ 0:                   flip s
else if rand() < exp(−ΔE/T): flip s
```

The magic number falls out of Onsager's exact 1944 solution:

$$
T_c = \frac{2J}{\ln(1 + \sqrt{2})} \approx 2.269\,J
$$

Below $T_c$ the readout $|M|$, the average spin, climbs toward 1 as the lattice orders. Above $T_c$ it hovers near 0.

## Where it shows up

The Ising model is the fruit fly of statistical physics, and its reach goes well past magnets. The same "agree with your neighbours, but noise fights you" rule describes a binary alloy sorting its two metals, a liquid boiling into gas near its critical point, and simplified neurons that fire or rest. It also anchors universality: very different systems share identical critical exponents near their transition, so measuring one teaches you about the rest. Image denoising, spin glasses, and opinion-spreading models borrow the same structure.

## The knobs

- **Temperature T**: how much thermal jostling the spins feel. Cool below $T_c$ and domains lock in; heat above it and any order dissolves into noise. Park it near 2.27 to watch criticality itself.
- **Coupling J**: how strongly neighbours want to agree. Raising $J$ also raises $T_c$, so a stiffer coupling needs more heat to melt. Changing $J$ re-randomises the lattice for a clean start.
- **Field h**: an external bias. Push it positive and up-spins (orange) win even without much cooperation; push it negative and teal takes the sheet.
- **Speed**: how many spin flips run each frame. Turn it up to evolve the pattern faster, without touching the underlying physics.
