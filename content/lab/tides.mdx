## Two bulges, one spinning observer

The blue mesh is the equilibrium ocean, its bulge exaggerated about 4.2 million times. The white arrow points at the Moon, the amber arrow at the Sun. The faint rings are the solid Earth, turning beneath the fixed bulges through one lunar day of 24 h 50 min. The violet sphere is an observer riding the surface; the violet strip above the globe is the water height under it across the day, with the faint line at the undisturbed level $\zeta = 0$.

### Five experiments

1. Select **Spring tide, new Moon**. The equatorial range reads 0.781 m and the observer at 45° sees two equal highs of 0.130 m, 12.4 h apart.
2. Drag the Sun–Moon angle to 90°. The range falls to 0.289 m, then returns to 0.781 m at 180°: the far-side bulge is as real as the near one.
3. Select **Lunar standstill**. With the Moon 28.5° above the equator the highs become 0.355 m and −0.094 m: one "high" tide sits below the undisturbed sphere.
4. From there, set the Sun–Moon angle to 90°. The solar term swallows the smaller lunar high; the readout reports one high per lunar day.
5. Select **Equatorial neap**, then drag Moon distance from 406.7 to 356.5. The lunar amplitude climbs from 0.301 m to 0.447 m: the inverse-cube law in one slider.

The height is a sum of two second-degree Legendre terms,

$$
\zeta = \frac{M_m}{M_E}\left(\frac{R}{d_m}\right)^3 R\,P_2(\cos\psi_m) + \frac{M_s}{M_E}\left(\frac{R}{d_s}\right)^3 R\,P_2(\cos\psi_s),
\qquad P_2(x) = \frac{3x^2 - 1}{2},
$$

with $\psi$ the angle from the observer's zenith to each body. The amplitudes are 0.357 m for the Moon and 0.164 m for the Sun, although the Sun's direct pull is 178.7 times stronger.

### What the model leaves out

This is Newton's static equilibrium tide, not a harbour prediction: real oceans lag the Moon by hours and reach 16 m ranges in resonant bays. The Sun stays in the equatorial plane with the Sun–Moon angle frozen through the day, and solid-Earth yielding, which cuts the observed tide to roughly 0.69 of this figure, is omitted.
